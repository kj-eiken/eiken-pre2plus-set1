Birds and Glass Windows

Birds are some of the most skilled flyers in nature. A small city bird can fly at speeds of more than sixty kilometers an hour, and it can change direction in the air in a split second. Their wings are light but strong, so many birds can stay in the sky for hours without resting and can cross whole countries. 18, birds have very sharp eyes. Many of them can spot a tiny insect from far away, and some can even see colors that humans cannot see at all.

Even so, accidents between birds and glass buildings are reported in cities around the world. The majority of these crashes take place 19. At those times, large groups of birds travel between their summer and winter homes. Many collisions happen on sunny days and near buildings with large glass walls. Studies show that birds usually avoid solid walls and dark corners. Yet a bright glass wall can look like open sky, and birds often do not slow down before they hit it.

Another reason for these accidents is 20. Clear glass lets birds see trees and flowers on the other side of a room. Some glass also reflects the blue sky and white clouds like a mirror. A bird flying toward it cannot tell that a hard wall is there. Because of this, birds often hit windows without any warning, and many cities now ask builders to use special glass with small patterns that birds can see easily.

The Secret of Gecko Feet

The gecko is a small lizard that lives in warm countries, from Southeast Asia to parts of Africa. Many people keep geckos as pets because they are quiet and easy to care for. 21 being kept at home, geckos have long been seen as a sign of good luck in some countries. Some families even believe that a gecko on the wall protects their house. Scientists today are paying attention to another remarkable feature of this animal.

Gecko feet have a surprising ability. They can 22. A gecko can run up a glass wall and even walk upside down on a ceiling. Each toe has millions of tiny hairs, and these hairs are too small to see without a microscope. When a toe touches a surface, every hair pulls on it very lightly, but together they are strong. This lets the gecko hold on firmly without any glue, and it can lift its foot again with almost no effort.

The gecko's grip has 23. Engineers have borrowed this idea to make new kinds of tape, gloves, and robot hands. A few of these products can be used many times without losing their grip, and some can even be washed in water and used again. Others can carry heavy glass panels, which makes work at construction sites easier and safer for workers. Scientists are still testing even better versions. In this way, a small part of an animal has given people many new ideas for solving everyday problems.

A
From:Laura Mitchell <l.mitchell@lakeview-science.org>
To:Ethan Brooks <ethan.brooks@mailbox-net.com>
Date:September 12
Subject:Invitation to Young Inventors Day

Dear Ethan,

This is Laura Mitchell from the Lakeview Science Museum. Thank you for taking my call last week. I was happy to tell you that your robot arm, which you made from recycled materials, was selected for our special event because of its clever design. Today, I am writing because the museum has finally set the date, and I would very much like you to take part.

The event will start in the early afternoon on September 27 in Orion Hall. Other young inventors, their parents, and some teachers and museum staff will join. On stage, you will show your invention to the audience and get a chance to talk with a professor from a local university. A reporter from the museum's magazine will also interview you for a few minutes, and parts of what you say will appear in next month's issue.

I have one more thing to ask. We want as many visitors as possible to see your robot arm, so we hope to keep it on display near the museum entrance for a month after the event. This will let families learn about recycling and may encourage young visitors to build things themselves. Please tell me if this is all right with you, so we can prepare a stand and a sign for it.

Thank you,
Laura Mitchell

B
Traveling to See the Stars

Astrotourism means traveling to places with very dark night skies in order to enjoy the stars. Many of these places are in mountains, deserts, or small islands far from big cities. During these trips, travelers can take part in activities such as watching the planets through a telescope, listening to a guide talk about space, or sleeping outside under the open sky. More than just a sightseeing trip, astrotourism gives people a chance to learn about the universe while they enjoy quiet nature.

Astrotourism can be good for visitors and for local people alike. Visitors, who often come from busy cities, can feel calm and relaxed under a quiet sky and give their minds a rest. At the same time, small towns can earn extra money by running hotels, tours, and cafés for the visitors. Some towns also protect their dark skies by using softer street lights, which helps the local wildlife, too. In this way, astrotourism helps people and nature at the same time.

Astrotourism has grown in popularity for several reasons, particularly among young people. First, city lights have become so bright that many people can hardly see any stars from their homes. They want to find places where the night sky still looks the way it did long ago. Second, social media plays a big role. Beautiful pictures of the Milky Way are shared online every day, and these photos make many viewers want to see it with their own eyes.

A recent survey in one country found that the number of people joining star-watching tours was about three times higher in 2024 than in 2018. In addition, many travelers now stay for several nights instead of just one evening. In some popular areas, hotels near dark places are fully booked months before the best seasons. These trends suggest that as modern cities grow brighter, more and more people long for the stars and for quiet places far away.
